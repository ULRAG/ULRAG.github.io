An Overview of Religious Freedom in Educational Institutions: From the 1960s to Now

By Kaitlyn Carannante — January 28, 2022

The debate on how religion fits into educational institutions has been a long and messy one. In the 1960s, the courts made the first ruling against religion in public schools by prohibiting the use of the Bible and time for prayer in the Engel v. Vitale case. The presence of Bibles and prayer in public schools dates back to when public schools were institutionalized in the 1820s (Lynch, The Edvocate). The issue, however, with allowing these practices was that America was becoming more diverse religiously and not all religions could be taught in every public school. If public schools were to continue only teaching one religion it would be viewed as an infringement on other religious followers’ First Amendment rights. Parents did have an alternative, though. If a parent wished for their children to continue learning about religion in school they had the option to send them to a private religious school. Today some states give tuition vouchers that allow parents to choose where their children will attend school, but many states do not allow the money to go toward sectarian schools.

In 2002 the city of Cleveland, Ohio put a voucher program in place that allowed access to sectarian schools. There were multiple demographic groups that did not approve of this program, and the American Civil Liberties Union and others took them to court. The Supreme Court ruled in favor of the voucher program in Zelman v. Simmons-Harris. Chief Justice Rehnquist stated that the voucher program was "entirely neutral with respect to religion" and "permits genuine choice among options public and private, secular and religious. The program is, therefore, a program of true private choice" (Zelman v. Simmons-Harris).

In 2020 there was a similar issue. Kendra Espinoza, a mother in Montana, registered for a scholarship for her students to attend a Christian school. Shortly after the scholarship program was created, the state limited eligible schools to nonsectarian ones, citing a provision of the state constitution prohibiting public funding of religiously affiliated programs (Espinoza v. Montana Dept. of Revenue, Oyez). In a 5–4 decision, the Supreme Court ruled that the restriction violated the Free Exercise Clause, which “protects religious observers against unequal treatment” and against “laws that impose special disabilities on the basis of religious status.”

Fast forward to the present day: in Maine, 260 districts do not have their own high schools. To fill this gap, the state offers tuition assistance for private or out-of-district public schools. When three families—The Carsons, Gillises, and Nelsons—chose sectarian private schools, their funding was denied. They sued, arguing that the decision violated their constitutional rights. After losing in district court and at the U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, they appealed to the Supreme Court (Carson v. Makin), which granted certiorari on July 2, 2021 and scheduled oral arguments for December 8, 2021.

In my opinion, I think that the Court will vote in favor of the three sets of parents. Based on how the justices ruled in Espinoza, it seems likely they will view Maine’s restriction as a violation of the Free Exercise Clause. Given the continuity of the Court’s makeup since Espinoza—minus Justice Ginsburg, who dissented—it makes sense this case may also be decided in favor of allowing public tuition aid at sectarian schools.
