Gender Disparity in Elite Law Firm Hiring Practices

Madison Bell

November 17th, 2025

Introduction

In 2016, a survey done by the Florida Bar’s Young Lawyers Division revealed that 43% of women experienced gender bias within the legal profession. This included salary discrepancies, lack of opportunities for advancement, and insensitive comments by colleagues, partners, or clients (Implicit Bias in the Legal Profession, p. 8). The findings of this survey reflect the gender biases and inequalities that are rooted in a profession which prides itself on fairness and justice.

There are many broader implications that stem from gender disparity in the legal field. These implications go beyond people’s individual careers and onto the broader structures of justice, representation, and equity. When women are underrepresented in the legal profession, specifically in leadership roles, important perspectives in issues such as family law, workplace discrimination, and gender-based violence may be overlooked. This gender imbalance in the legal field can perpetuate systemic biases towards women and hinder the development of laws that protect all citizens male and female. Deborah L. Rhode states, “Gender diversity in the legal profession is not just about fairness; it is about improving the quality of justice delivered. When women are underrepresented in law, particularly in positions of power, entire perspectives are lost.”

Although women today make up 38% of attorneys—a significant increase from the 5% representation in the 1950s—barriers to leadership roles for women in elite law firms still remain (Fenwick 2023). This paper examines the underrepresentation of women in elite law firms by exploring implicit and explicit biases, structural barriers within the legal profession, and caregiving responsibilities. These factors collectively perpetuate gender disparities in the field, and this paper also explores actions that can foster greater equality going forward.

Historical Context & Current State

Historically, the legal profession has been dominated by men. In 1950, women made up less than 5% of all attorneys (Fenwick 2023). This began to shift after the feminist movements of the 1960s and 1970s. By 2022, women accounted for 38% of lawyers, reflecting progress but also showing slow growth over the last decade. As Fenwick (2023) notes, “The last decade’s modest growth suggests that the journey to equality is far from over.”

Today, women still face significant challenges in reaching leadership roles. Liebenberg and Scharf (2019) highlight high attrition rates among experienced women in elite law firms. Their research shows that 58% of women cited caregiving responsibilities as a primary reason for leaving. They explain that women disproportionately bear responsibilities for childcare, scheduling, and family logistics. These ongoing challenges indicate that although women have gained greater access to the legal field, advancement remains hindered by additional obstacles.

Implicit & Explicit Biases in Hiring

Implicit biases—unconscious attitudes shaping behavior—play a major role in perpetuating gender inequality in elite law firms. Whysall (2018) states that implicit cognition influences decisions at every stage of talent management, from recruitment to promotions. Decision-makers often rely on heuristics and stereotypical assumptions, especially ideas of leadership being tied to masculine traits. These biases disadvantage women by making them seem less aligned with traditional expectations of a “successful attorney.”

Explicit biases, though less overt today, also influence hiring. Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) found that applicants with names perceived as female or associated with racial minorities received significantly fewer callbacks, even when qualifications were identical. “White names receive 50 percent more callbacks,” the authors write. This highlights not only biases against women but a compounded disadvantage for women of color.

Within law specifically, Coontz (1995) found that women consistently report discrimination in pay and advancement opportunities, while many men fail to recognize these inequities. This awareness gap perpetuates systemic barriers, especially since those in power—primarily men—may not perceive the need for reform.

Structural & Cultural Barriers

Elite law firms operate within demanding work structures often incompatible with work-life balance. Liebenberg and Scharf (2019) note that women frequently leave firms because of overwhelming workloads, expectations to bill large numbers of hours, and responsibilities like business origination. These structural demands are heightened by cultural expectations and caregiving roles that disproportionately fall on women.

Workplace cultures also prioritize presenteeism and after-hours networking, which disproportionately exclude women with caregiving duties. According to the Intellectual Property Owners Association, women are often held to higher ethical standards and face harsher consequences than men for similar offenses. These norms create environments in which women must exert significantly more effort to achieve equivalent recognition.

Counterpoints & Broader Implications

Some may argue gender disparities are fading due to increasing numbers of women entering the legal field. However, data shows that while more women join the profession, they do not advance at the same rate as men. Whysall (2018) notes that the few women who reach senior ranks often become “tokens,” facing heightened scrutiny.

These disparities affect more than just the legal profession. Diverse legal teams bring broader perspectives and more innovative solutions. Ensuring gender equity strengthens professional excellence and creates models for other industries, helping dismantle systemic inequalities beyond law.

Solutions & Recommendations

To address inequities, several organizational reforms could be implemented. Anonymous hiring practices that conceal personal identifiers such as name and gender could reduce implicit bias in applicant evaluation. Whysall (2018) notes that blind screening and joint evaluation—where candidates are compared in batches—can reduce reliance on stereotypes.

Goldin and Rouse (2000) support the effectiveness of blind evaluations, demonstrating that screens in orchestra auditions increased women's advancement by 50%. Similarly, Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) showed that removing racial identifiers could reduce disparities, suggesting blind hiring may improve gender equity in law.

Policy changes are also crucial. More flexible work arrangements—including remote work, parental leave, and clear promotion criteria—support women with caregiving responsibilities. Liebenberg and Scharf (2019) identify flexible scheduling, paid parental leave, and part-time partnership policies as vital to retaining senior women.

Educational initiatives such as mandatory implicit bias training can raise awareness among hiring managers. Tracking metrics like gender distribution in leadership roles and pay gaps would hold firms accountable for disparities.

Potential Limitations & Barriers

Implementing reforms faces several challenges. Those who benefit from the current system may resist change, weakening bias training initiatives. Implicit biases are deeply ingrained and difficult to eliminate, even with awareness. Joan Williams describes “Prove-It-Again” bias, where women and people of color must repeatedly demonstrate competence to receive the same recognition.

Cultural norms around caregiving also persist. Women, especially mothers, are often perceived as less committed to their careers, contributing to what scholars call the “Maternal Wall.” This bias leads to assumptions about competence and dedication, hindering advancement.

Conclusion

The underrepresentation of women in elite law firms is a multifaceted issue rooted in implicit and explicit biases, structural barriers, and cultural expectations. Although progress has been made, significant work remains to achieve equity, particularly in leadership roles.

Addressing disparities requires organizational reforms, policy changes, and educational initiatives. Creating inclusive environments would not only empower women but also strengthen the legal profession. Future research should further explore intersectional challenges faced by women of color and evaluate the long-term effects of implemented reforms.
